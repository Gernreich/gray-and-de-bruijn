Gray and de Bruijn

Two classic ways to walk through a small alphabet, tried on three alphabets from earlier projects: the A B C layers of atomic layer memory, the sixteen truth tables of Zellweger's Logic Alphabet, and the 28 letters of the grid script. Four of the walks are cut files for the laser.

Whose ideas these are. The layer memory and the grid script are the author's. The Logic Alphabet is Shea Zellweger's. Gray codes are named after Frank Gray of Bell Labs (patented 1953), though Émile Baudot used one in the 1870s. De Bruijn sequences are named after Nicolaas de Bruijn (1946), who was preceded by Camille Flye Sainte-Marie (1894). Rulers and discs that read their own position this way are standard in absolute encoders. What is new here is putting the three alphabets through these walks. Claude (Anthropic), working in Claude Code, proposed this exploration at Michael's request. Claude also worked out the counts and the two findings below (the rings that put each truth table across from its negation, and the bound on the grid script), designed the pieces, and wrote walks.py, the cut files, the 3D views and this page. walks.py checks every count.

Two ways to walk an alphabet

A Gray code lists every symbol once, and each differs from the one before by a single change. Counting 0 to 15 in binary, 0111 to 1000 flips four bits at once. In Gray code no step flips more than one. If the list closes back on itself, it is a Gray cycle.

A de Bruijn sequence is a single cyclic string in which every word of n symbols occurs exactly once. Slide a window n wide along it and you never see the same thing twice, so what you see tells you where you are. For two symbols and n = 5 it is 32 long: {{DISC}}.

A ruler that knows where it is

The bottom edge of this 300 mm ruler is a track of 1 mm cells, engraved or bare, cut from the de Bruijn sequence for windows of nine. Any nine neighbouring cells occur at only one place on it. Lay the reader card on the track, read the nine cells through its window, and the pattern names the millimetre where the window starts: 301 positions, from 0 to 300. The ordinary scale on the top edge is there to check against.

Two discs, one question

Both discs give 32 angles, one per 11.25°. The Gray disc uses five rings, read along one radius from the hole outwards. Turning past any boundary changes only one ring, so a reader caught on a boundary is never off by more than one step (in plain binary, 15 to 16 would change all five rings). The de Bruijn disc uses one ring of 32 cells and reads five neighbouring cells clockwise. One ring and five readers do the job of five rings and one reader.

Layer memory

In atomic layer memory a bit is the site of a close-packed layer, 0 on A and 1 on C, with a spacer on B between every two bits. Writing the next number means moving the layers that changed. Counting from 0 to 15 in binary moves 26 layers. In Gray code it moves 15, exactly one per step. Moved layers are ringed in yellow:

A stack with every window once

A close-packed layer can never sit on the same site as the layer below, so of the 27 words of three layers only 12 can exist (3 × 2 × 2). The stack below contains each of those 12 exactly once, read upwards in windows of three and wrapping round (the dashed cells repeat the start). The same holds for any window size n: such a stack is 3 × 2n−1 layers long, and walks.py builds and checks the ones for windows of 3, 4 and 5.

{{STACK_DB}}

The same twelve layers as close-packed atoms, the bottom layer at the bottom. The three bright layers are the current window; the others are dimmed. Each atom nests in a hollow of the layer below, so no layer repeats the site under it. Drag to turn it, scroll to zoom.

A second way to read a stack

Because neighbouring layers always differ, each layer is one of two things relative to the layer below it: a turn up (A→B, B→C, C→A) or a turn down. Writing up as 1 and down as 0, the stack above, once round, reads {{STACK_DB_STEPS}}. This is the Hägg notation crystallographers use for close-packed polytypes. It stores one bit in every layer, twice the density of the spacer code. The cost is that a bit depends on two layers, so misreading one layer corrupts the bits on both sides of it. The spacer code reads every bit off a single layer.

The Logic Alphabet as a ring

A Logic Alphabet glyph stands on a square of four places, one per row of the truth table (TT TF FT FF, set out as in Logic Research), with a stem wherever the function is true. A Gray code through the sixteen glyphs therefore adds or removes one stem at a time. Closed into a ring, that is a Hamiltonian cycle on the four-dimensional cube, and there are 1344 of them. 48 of them are antipodal: every glyph sits directly across the ring from its negation, the glyph with every stem switched. The same ring on the four-dimensional cube

Give each row of the truth table its own axis, TT, TF, FT and FF. The sixteen glyphs are then the corners of a four-dimensional cube, and each step of the ring runs along one edge. Below, the cube is turned in four dimensions and drawn in perspective, the way a cube's shadow is drawn on paper. Grey lines are the cube's 32 edges, and the 16 yellow ones are the ring. The two markers travel the ring half a turn apart. The line between them always passes through the centre, because each glyph lies opposite its negation.

Drag to turn it in three dimensions, scroll to zoom. On its own it keeps turning in the fourth dimension too.

The grid script as a ring

A grid-script letter is two steps among the eight grid directions, traced either way round. The tightest Gray code would change one step by an eighth of a turn each time. That is impossible, and the reason is parity. Call the turn between a letter's two steps its bend. Turning one step by an eighth changes the bend by one, so such a move always goes between an even-bend letter and an odd-bend letter. There are 12 even-bend letters ({{EVEN}}) and 16 odd-bend ones ({{ODD}}). A cycle that alternates between the two kinds needs the same number of each, so no cycle uses eighth-turns alone.

The ring therefore allows any change to one step and counts the turning. A cycle through all 28 can alternate kinds at most 24 times, and each of the 4 remaining moves stays within one kind and costs at least a quarter turn. That makes 24 + 4 × 2 = 32 eighths at the least, and the ring below reaches it. The stroke drawn in yellow is the one that turned on the way in from the letter before. The dot marks where the pen starts.

Cutting

The six cut files are in cut-files/, each one fitting the 600 × 305 mm bed of the xTool P2S. They use the staircase ruler's layer colours: green #008000 to engrave (filled), blue #0000ff to score, orange #ff8000 for inner cuts (cut first), black #000000 for outlines. Outlines sit half a kerf outside the finished edge, so the ruler's zero is the cut face. python3 walks.py --kerf=0.18 rewrites the files for another kerf (the default is 0.13).
